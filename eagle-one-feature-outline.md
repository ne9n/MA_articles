## 2. From experiments to the Eagle One

*Suggested length: 150-175 words*

- In 2023, the team experimented with foam and Coroplast electric trainers in tractor configuration, using a radio-control transmitter to manage throttle. The arrangement felt cumbersome, and motor shafts bent in crashes during sport flying.
- Explain the impact problem: control-line trainers can dive nose-first into hard ground, exposing a nose-mounted electric motor's propeller shaft to damage. The team observed that the glow-powered trainers' propeller and shaft arrangement was more rigid; confirm the comparison and its limits before presenting it as a general claim.
- Note that Larry Regner designed a similar trainer used on hard surfaces or indoors, with a radio-control transmitter for throttle control. At KidVenture's high flight volume, the team concluded that transmitter-based throttle management would not suit its operation. Describe this as a difference in operating requirements, not a criticism of Regner's design.
- Explain how those experiences informed the move toward a twin-boom pusher and protecting the motor from impacts, without retelling the later timer's development.
- Introduce Chris Sterner's Eagle One and trace its development through prototype and introduction.
- **Reporting needed:** Ask Sterner and the team who initiated the project, what constraints they set, and what changed during early testing. Confirm the exact motor location and sequence of events; verify Regner's trainer name, operating context, and any direct influence on the Eagle One.

## 3. Defining the design mission

*Suggested length: 200-225 words*

- Translate the outreach mission into design requirements: manageable handling for beginners, resistance to rough arrivals, dependable operation, and practical servicing by volunteers.
- Discuss how experience with Norvel .061-powered trainers helped establish a useful wing-area range of approximately 220-250 square inches, and how the design also had to fit available Coroplast sheets. Two sheets can make three trainers; confirm sheet dimensions and the assumed 250-square-inch upper end.
- **Power estimate to verify:** The suggested Norvel .061 output of 150-175 watts has not been independently confirmed. Identify the exact engine variant, a reliable power rating, and the conditions under which it was measured before including a number. Engine shaft output and electric motor input are not directly interchangeable.
- Explore where those needs compete: for example, durability versus weight or easy handling versus performance in wind. Treat these as questions for the designer, not established conclusions.
- **Reporting needed:** Ask which requirements were nonnegotiable, how they were prioritized, and what constraints affected the final design.

## 4. Design features and the reasons behind them

*Suggested length: 500-600 words*

Organize this section around design decisions and their practical consequences rather than assembly instructions.

- **Twin-boom pusher configuration:** Explain why moving the motor away from the nose helps protect its shaft during nose-first impacts. Describe how the arrangement evolved into a twin-boom design: the booms roughly divide the airplane into thirds and accommodate a 7-inch nylon propeller. Explore the resulting design choices and tradeoffs, including balance, propeller clearance, and service access.
- **Balance and beginner handling:** Explain that the rear-mounted motor required forward battery placement to achieve a reasonable center of gravity; the battery slides into a pocket. The center of gravity is deliberately far forward to limit control response for beginners; confirm its location and the handling tradeoffs with the designer.
- **Hand-launch safety:** Contrast the launcher's position behind a tractor propeller with the need for added awareness around a pusher propeller. The booms were made tall to discourage hand placement near the propeller, and propeller awareness is always part of the safety briefing. Describe the booms as an aid, not a substitute for safe handling.
- **Low-speed launch behavior:** Discuss the team's concern that thrust applied behind the center of gravity can contribute to the airplane pitching or flipping at low speed, in contrast with a tractor's tendency to "hang on the propeller." Confirm the observed behavior and contributing thrust-line and aerodynamic factors before explaining the mechanism or attributing it solely to motor location.
- **Size and layout:** Introduce the 30-1/4-inch wingspan and 225-square-inch wing area. Connect the sizing to experience with the earlier trainers and available sheet stock, without presenting the unverified engine power estimate as fact.
- **Coroplast airframe:** Explain the use of approximately 4 mm fluted polypropylene and the practical material yield of three trainers from two sheets. Ask how the material, structure, and joining approach support durability and maintenance. Discuss limitations as well as benefits.
- **Control system and handling:** Explain the relevant control components for less-experienced readers, concentrating on design and tuning choices rather than duplicating Part 1's student briefing.
- **Electric power and integration:** Identify the 2807 1300 kV brushless motor. Explore how the motor and its placement fit the airframe and mission, rather than retelling Part 1's account of the transition. Reserve detailed power-control electronics and operational profiles for Part 3.
- **Timer integration:** If needed for context, briefly identify the Cheesehead Timer's role and credit Dave Siegler. The timer and electronic speed controller (ESC) are attached to the wing with Velcro; in a crash, the battery and ESC can disconnect. Describe this as a possible outcome, not a guaranteed safety mechanism. Leave the electronics' development and operation for Part 3.
- **Landing and serviceability:** Explain confirmed design provisions for arrivals and field maintenance, focusing on why they were chosen rather than repeating the flight routine.
